How to Start a Podcast in 2026: The Complete Step-by-Step Guide

Published August 2026 · 14-minute read · Category: Content Creation

Over 500 million people listen to podcasts worldwide, and the medium is still growing. Yet most people who want to start a podcast never publish a single episode — not because it's hard, but because they get stuck on equipment, hosting, format, or what to actually say into the microphone. The truth is that learning how to start a podcast is mostly a planning problem, not a technical one. You can record a professional-sounding first episode this weekend for under $150.

This guide walks through every step: choosing your niche and format, picking a name, buying only the equipment you actually need, selecting a host, recording and editing, launching with a batch of episodes, and setting up the systems that keep you consistent. By the end, you'll have a concrete plan to go from idea to published — without the trial and error that stalls most would-be podcasters.

Key takeaway: Starting a podcast in 2026 requires four decisions and one system — your niche, your format, your equipment, your host, and a planner that tracks episodes, guests, and content so you don't quit after episode 7. The biggest reason podcasts fail isn't bad audio; it's inconsistent publishing, which is a planning problem.

Step 1: Choose Your Niche and Format

Every successful podcast has a clearly defined niche — a specific audience with a specific problem or interest. "A podcast about marketing" is too broad. "A podcast about email marketing for solo SaaS founders" is a niche. The narrower the niche, the easier it is to find listeners, book guests, and stand out in Apple Podcasts categories.

Run your idea through three filters:

  1. Audience size: Is there a community already talking about this? Search Reddit, Facebook groups, and existing podcasts. If there are 5+ active podcasts in the niche, that's a good sign — it means there's demand. Zero podcasts usually means zero demand.
  2. Your edge: What makes your take different? It could be your background (e.g. "a former Spotify data scientist explains the music industry"), your format ("15-minute tactical episodes"), or your angle ("interviewing founders who failed and came back").
  3. Sustainability: Can you produce 50 episodes on this topic without running out of things to say? If you can list 50 episode ideas in 20 minutes, the niche is sustainable. If you struggle to list 10, it's too narrow.

Pick a Format

Format determines your production workload and your equipment needs. The three core formats:

  • Solo monologue: Lowest barrier — one mic, no scheduling, full control. Best for tactical, opinion-driven, or storytelling shows. Hardest part: you carry every episode.
  • Interview: Guests bring content and audience. You need reliable scheduling, a guest pipeline, and decent remote recording. Best for network-building shows.
  • Co-hosted conversation: Two or more hosts banter on a topic. Natural and low-prep, but you need chemistry and a co-host who shows up every week.

If you're starting alone and unsure, begin with solo episodes. You can always add interviews later. The worst choice is no choice — pick a format and commit to 10 episodes before switching.

Step 2: Name, Cover, and Branding

Your podcast name should be searchable, memorable, and ideally contain your core keyword. "The Tim Ferriss Show" works because of the host's fame; for a new show, a descriptive name ranks better. "Indie SaaS Founders" or "The Email Marketing Show" tells a listener (and Apple's search algorithm) exactly what the show is about in three seconds.

Name rules:

  • Under 30 characters if possible — long names get truncated in podcast apps.
  • Include your main keyword ("podcast," "show," or the topic) for search visibility.
  • Check Apple Podcasts, Spotify, and domain registries for name collisions.
  • Avoid clever spellings that make you hard to search for ("PodKastr" loses listeners).

Your cover art is the single biggest factor in whether someone clicks your show in a directory. Use a 3000×3000px image (Apple's requirement), high contrast, large readable text (the title must be legible at 55×55px in a podcast app), and no more than two fonts. If you're not a designer, use Canva's podcast cover templates — they're free and tuned for the format. A bad cover will sink a good show.

Step 3: Equipment (Only What You Need)

This is where most beginners overspend. You do not need a $400 mixer, four XLR microphones, or a soundproof booth. The audio quality that matters is clarity and absence of echo — both achievable with a single USB microphone in a quiet, soft-furnished room.

The beginner setup that produces broadcast-quality audio:

  • Microphone: Samson Q2U (~$70) or Audio-Technica ATR2100x (~$99). Both are USB/XLR hybrid dynamic mics — dynamic mics reject room noise better than condensers, which matters in untreated rooms.
  • Headphones: Any closed-back pair ($30–$60). You need them to monitor recording and avoid echo on remote calls. Avoid earbuds — they leak audio into the mic.
  • Boom arm: ~$20. Optional but worth it — keeps the mic at mouth height and reduces desk thumps.
  • Pop filter: ~$10. Reduces plosives (the harsh "p" and "b" bursts).
  • Software: Audacity (free, Mac/Windows/Linux) or GarageBand (free, Mac). For remote interviews, use Riverside.fm (free tier records 2 hours/month in 720p audio).

Recording environment matters more than gear. A closet full of clothes is a surprisingly good vocal booth — the fabric absorbs reflections. If that's not practical, record in a room with soft furniture, close the windows, turn off fans, and keep the mic 6 inches from your mouth. A $70 mic in a quiet room beats a $400 mic in an echoing kitchen.

You upload one file; the host handles the rest. Do not host audio on your own website — bandwidth costs and reliability will hurt you.

Step 5: Record and Edit Your First Episodes

Before you launch, record a batch of three episodes. A batch launch gives listeners multiple episodes to binge on day one, which signals to Apple's algorithm that your show has engagement — and engagement drives chart placement.

Recording workflow:

  1. Write a loose outline (not a full script — you'll sound robotic). Bullet points for intro, 3–5 main points, and outro.
  2. Record a 10-second room-tone clip (silence with the mic on) before you start — useful for noise reduction in editing.
  3. Hit record, do a 5-second sound check ("testing 1, 2, 3"), then start talking.
  4. If you stumble, pause for 3 seconds, then redo the line. The silence makes cuts easy to find in editing.
  5. Keep episodes 20–45 minutes for your first batch — long enough to deliver value, short enough to edit without despair.

Editing in Audacity: import the audio, run Noise Reduction (select the room-tone clip as the noise profile, apply to the whole track), cut long pauses and stumbles, normalize to -16 LUFS (Apple's loudness standard), and export as MP3 at 128kbps mono (or 192kbps stereo). That's 90% of podcast editing — you don't need fancy plugins. If you outsource, expect $30–$80 per episode from a freelance editor on Fiverr or Upwork.

A common mistake: spending 5 hours editing a 30-minute episode. Aim for a 1:1 edit ratio — 30 minutes of audio, roughly 30–45 minutes of editing. Perfectionism kills consistency, and consistency beats polish in podcasting.

Step 6: Show Notes and Metadata

Show notes are how new listeners find you via search — both in podcast apps and on Google. Write a 150–300 word summary for each episode that includes the episode's main takeaway, any links or resources mentioned, and a call to action (subscribe, leave a review, or visit a URL). Include a keyword-rich episode title: "How a Solo Founder Hit $20K MRR" beats "Episode 12."

Each episode needs:

  • Title: Under 60 characters, benefit-driven or curiosity-driven, includes a keyword.
  • Description: 2–3 sentences that Apple/Spotify show in the episode list.
  • Show notes: 150–300 words, links, timestamps, CTA.
  • Transcript: Full text — boosts SEO and accessibility. Use Otter.ai or Whisper for free transcription.

Step 7: Launch with a Batch

The launch is your one chance to make Apple's "New & Noteworthy" chart, which drives a wave of free discovery. The recipe:

  1. Publish 3 episodes on day one. Listeners who like episode 1 will binge 2 and 3, and Apple's algorithm reads that as engagement.
  2. Publish episode 4 and 5 on schedule in weeks 2 and 3. Consistency in the first month signals an active show.
  3. Ask your existing audience (email list, social, friends) to subscribe and leave a rating in the first 7 days. Apple weights early ratings heavily for chart placement.
  4. Promote each episode with a 30-second audiogram (use Headliner.app — free) on social.

Do not launch with one episode. A single-episode show has no binge potential, no chart signal, and no reason for a listener to subscribe yet. Three is the minimum effective dose.

How Podcasts Make Money

Monetization comes later for most shows, but it helps to know the models so you can design for them from episode one. The five main revenue streams:

  • Sponsorships / ad reads: The most common model. CPM-based — you're paid per 1,000 downloads. A midroll ad on a show with 1,000 downloads earns roughly $18–$25. Host-read ads outperform pre-recorded ads 3:1 because listeners trust the host's voice. Most shows need 1,000+ downloads/episode before sponsors come to them.
  • Affiliate marketing: Recommend products with affiliate links in your show notes. Works from episode one, even with a tiny audience, because you're paid per conversion not per download.
  • Listener support: Patreon, Buy Me a Coffee, or Apple Podcasts Subscriptions. Best for niche shows with devoted audiences — a few hundred loyal listeners can fund a show.
  • Premium episodes: Paid bonus episodes or ad-free feeds via Apple Podcasts Subscriptions or Patreon. Works once listeners are hooked on the free show.
  • Your own products/services: The highest-margin option. A podcast is a marketing channel for a course, consulting, book, or SaaS. Many 6-figure podcasts earn more from the host's product than from sponsorships.

If you have a product to sell, start there — affiliate and sponsorship income can follow once downloads grow. The Podcast Planner includes a Sponsorships tab that tracks every deal by type, status, and revenue so you can see at a glance what's monetizing.

5 Mistakes That Kill New Podcasts

1. Launching with One Episode

A single episode gives listeners nothing to binge and Apple no engagement signal. Fix: Always launch with 3 episodes on day one, then publish weekly.

2. Inconsistent Publishing

Missing weeks signals to directories (and listeners) that the show is dying. Fix: Pick a cadence you can sustain — weekly, biweekly, or monthly — and stick to it for at least 20 episodes before changing. Use a content calendar to plan 3 months ahead.

3. Bad Cover Art

A muddy, low-contrast cover with tiny text is invisible in podcast apps. Fix: 3000×3000px, two fonts max, readable at thumbnail size. Test it at 55×55px before publishing.

4. No Guest Pipeline

If you book guests one at a time, you'll run dry and panic-publish. Fix: Maintain a guest pipeline with at least 5 confirmed guests and 20 leads at all times. A planner makes this visible.

5. Treating It as a Hobby, Not a System

Podcasters who don't track episodes, guests, and content in one place quit. Fix: Use a planner from day one. The cost of a spreadsheet ($11) is trivial compared to the cost of quitting after 7 episodes you spent months recording.
