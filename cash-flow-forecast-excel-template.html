What Is a Cash Flow Forecast?

A cash flow forecast is a financial projection that estimates when money will enter and leave your business over a specific period. Unlike a profit and loss statement (which shows whether you're profitable on paper), a cash flow forecast shows whether you'll actually have cash in the bank when bills are due.

The difference matters. If you invoice a client $10,000 in March but they pay in May, your P&L shows revenue in March — but your bank account is empty until May. A cash flow forecast accounts for that timing gap, which is the single most common reason profitable small businesses fail.

A cash flow forecast Excel template automates this process. Instead of manually building formulas for opening balances, closing balances, and cumulative cash, the template does the math for you. You enter your expected revenue and expenses; the spreadsheet handles the rest.

How to Build a Cash Flow Forecast in Excel (Step by Step)

If you want to understand how a cash flow forecast works under the hood, here's how to build one from scratch. (If you'd rather skip the setup, download the ready-made template and jump to the next section.)

Step 1: Set Up Your 12-Month Grid

Create a table with months across the top (Jan through Dec) and the following rows down the left side:

  • Opening Balance — cash in the bank at the start of the month
  • Cash Inflows — sales revenue, loan proceeds, investor capital, asset sales
  • Cash Outflows — rent, payroll, supplies, utilities, loan payments, taxes
  • Net Cash Flow — inflows minus outflows
  • Closing Balance — opening balance plus net cash flow

Step 2: Project Your Revenue

List every revenue stream your business has. For each, estimate monthly income based on historical data, signed contracts, or conservative growth assumptions. Be realistic — overestimating revenue is the most common forecasting mistake.

A good template separates revenue into categories (e.g., product sales, services, recurring subscriptions) so you can see which streams are growing and which are shrinking.

Step 3: List All Expenses (Fixed and Variable)

Split expenses into two categories:

  • Fixed expenses — rent, insurance, software subscriptions, loan payments (same every month)
  • Variable expenses — materials, contractor fees, marketing, shipping (fluctuate with sales volume)

This split matters because it feeds directly into your break-even analysis. Fixed costs don't change with sales; variable costs do.

Step 4: Add the Cash Flow Formula

The core formula is simple:

Closing Balance = Opening Balance + (Total Inflows − Total Outflows)

Then carry the closing balance to the next month:

Next Month's Opening Balance = This Month's Closing Balance

This creates a running cumulative cash position that shows your bank balance projection across all 12 months.

Step 5: Add Visual Charts

Two charts make the forecast instantly readable:

  • Net cash flow bar chart — shows which months are positive (green) and negative (red)
  • Cumulative cash line chart — shows your projected bank balance across the year, making it obvious when you'll dip below zero

Step 6: Add Break-Even and Runway Calculations

This is where most DIY templates stop — and where a professional template adds real value. Calculate:

  • Break-even units = Fixed Costs ÷ (Price per Unit − Variable Cost per Unit)
  • Break-even revenue = Break-even Units × Price per Unit
  • Margin of safety = (Actual Sales − Break-even Sales) ÷ Actual Sales
  • Monthly burn rate = Monthly Expenses − Monthly Revenue
  • Runway = Current Cash Balance ÷ Monthly Burn Rate
Pro tip: Build a sensitivity table that shows break-even at different price points and variable cost levels. This tells you how much room you have to discount or absorb cost increases before you stop being viable.

How to Use Break-Even Analysis to Make Better Decisions

Break-even analysis is the most underused tool in small business finance. It tells you the exact number of units (or dollars) you need to sell to cover all your costs. Below that number, you're losing money. Above it, you're profitable.

The Break-Even Formula

Break-even Units = Fixed Costs ÷ Contribution Margin

Where Contribution Margin = Selling Price − Variable Cost per Unit

A Real Example

Say you run a small e-commerce business:

  • Fixed costs (rent, software, insurance): $4,200/month
  • Average selling price per order: $65
  • Variable cost per order (product, shipping, payment fees): $28
  • Contribution margin: $65 − $28 = $37
  • Break-even units: $4,200 ÷ $37 = 114 orders/month
  • Break-even revenue: 114 × $65 = $7,410/month

Now you know: if you're getting fewer than 114 orders a month, you're burning cash. If you're above 114, every additional order adds $37 to your profit.

Understanding Cash Runway and Burn Rate

Runway is the metric that keeps founders and small business owners awake at night — in a good way. It tells you exactly how long your business can survive if revenue stops tomorrow.

The Runway Formula

Runway (months) = Current Cash Balance ÷ Monthly Burn Rate

Where Monthly Burn Rate = Monthly Expenses − Monthly Revenue

Example

If you have $48,000 in the bank, monthly expenses of $9,000, and monthly revenue of $3,000:

  • Burn rate: $9,000 − $3,000 = $6,000/month
  • Runway: $48,000 ÷ $6,000 = 8 months

This means you have 8 months to either increase revenue or cut costs before the money runs out. The Cash Flow Forecast & Break-Even Calculator's runway tab shows this automatically, along with an 18-month depletion timeline with visual progress bars that make the urgency obvious at a glance.

5 Common Cash Flow Forecasting Mistakes

  1. Overestimating revenue. Most founders project best-case sales. Use your slowest month as the baseline, not your best. A forecast that's too optimistic is worse than no forecast at all.
  2. Ignoring payment timing. If your clients pay net-30 or net-60, revenue earned in January might not hit your bank account until March. Your forecast must reflect when cash actually arrives, not when you invoice.
  3. Forgetting one-time expenses. Annual insurance premiums, equipment purchases, tax payments, and holiday bonuses can wipe out a month's cash. List them all.
  4. Not updating the forecast. A forecast made in January is useless by April if you haven't updated it with actual numbers. Review monthly.
  5. Only planning for one scenario. If you only model the base case, you're unprepared for downturns. Always run best, base, and worst cases — the scenario planner in the template makes this a one-click operation.

When to Update Your Cash Flow Forecast

A cash flow forecast is a living document. Here's a review schedule that works for most small businesses:

  • Monthly: Replace forecasted numbers with actuals. Adjust remaining months based on what happened.
  • Quarterly: Review your break-even point. Has it changed? Are fixed costs creeping up? Is your margin shrinking?
  • After major changes: New hire, price change, new product launch, lost major client — update the forecast immediately.
  • Before big decisions: Should you hire? Move to a bigger office? Invest in new equipment? Run the numbers through your forecast first.
