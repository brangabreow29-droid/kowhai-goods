Video Script Templates: Quick Answers

What is a video script template?

A fill-in-the-blank structure for a specific video format — a 15-second Short, a tutorial, a VSL. It tells you what to say in what order and how many words each section gets, so you start from structure instead of a blank page.

How do you write a video script?

Pick the template matching your format → write the hook first (8-12 words, the first 2 seconds) → fill the body beats to your word budget → read it aloud and time it, cutting 10-15% → add visual directions where B-roll and on-screen text go. Full walkthrough below.

How many words is a 60-second video script?

150-180 words. Long-form runs ~150 words per minute; fast-paced short-form runs 160-180. A 15-second script is 40-45 words, a 30-second script is 75-90 words, a 10-minute script is 1,400-1,500 words. Full table below.

What is the best video script template for Shorts and TikTok?

The three-line Hook-Value-CTA structure: hook (8-12 words, first 2 seconds), one idea of value, one CTA. Short-form dies from slow starts and multiple ideas — this template forbids both.

Two-column or single-column script format?

Two-column A/V format (audio left, visuals right) for videos with B-roll, demos, or client sign-off. Single-column for talking-head and teleprompter videos. Comparison table below.

Do I need a script for short-form videos?

Yes — short videos need scripts more than long ones, because 30 seconds has no room for a slow start. Experienced creators can use a 3-bullet outline, but the structure stays the same: hook, one idea, one CTA.

What Is a Video Script Template (and When You Need One)

A video script template is a pre-built structure for a specific video format: it names the sections your script needs (hook, beats, CTA), the order they run in, and the word budget for each. Instead of deciding what kind of video to write before you can write anything, you pick the template that matches your goal — teach, entertain, or sell — and fill it in.

You need a video script template whenever the video has a job beyond "talking to a camera": a Short that must retain past 3 seconds, a tutorial that must not ramble, a sponsor segment that must convert. The only videos that don't benefit from a template are live streams and casual stories — everything else gets measurably tighter, shorter, and easier to shoot from a template.

Twelve templates cover almost everything a working creator publishes. That sounds like a lot until you notice the pattern: every video script template is the same seven-part skeleton wearing different clothes.

The 7-Part Anatomy of Every Video Script

Every effective video script — regardless of format, platform, or length — is built from the same seven components:

  1. Hook (first 2 seconds, 8-12 words): the reason to keep watching, stated immediately. Never an intro, never "hey guys" — the payoff, named or teased.
  2. Context (1-2 sentences): just enough background for the promise to make sense. In short-form, this is one sentence or zero.
  3. Promise (1 sentence): what the viewer gets by the end. "By the end of this video you'll have a 30-day content calendar filled in."
  4. Body (3-7 beats): the delivery. One idea per beat, best material early — the middle of a video is where retention sags, so front-load.
  5. Transitions (2-4 words each): "which brings up…", "here's where it gets interesting". Transitions are retention repair — they reopen the loop the last beat closed.
  6. Payoff: deliver the promise completely. The viewer must be able to do something they couldn't before.
  7. CTA (1 sentence): exactly one action. Save, follow, click, comment. Two CTAs halve the response to each.
Key takeaway: a video script template is just this skeleton with the proportions pre-set. A 15-second Short keeps the hook, one beat, and the CTA — everything else gets cut. A 10-minute tutorial runs all seven at full size. When a video isn't working, diagnose it against the seven parts; the problem is almost always a weak hook or a missing payoff.

The Word-Count Math: How Many Words Per Minute of Video

You speak at roughly 150 words per minute in a natural long-form delivery, and 160-180 words per minute in the faster, punchier pace of Shorts and TikTok. Every video script template needs a word budget before you write, or you'll script a 45-second video and film 70 seconds of it:

RuntimeWord budgetTypical use
15 seconds40-45 wordsOne-tip Short, pre-roll teaser
30 seconds75-90 wordsTikTok sweet spot, pre-roll ad
60 seconds150-180 wordsShorts / Reels cap, sponsored segment
3 minutes420-450 wordsMini-tutorial, talking-head Short
8-12 minutes1,200-1,800 wordsYouTube long-form sweet spot
20 minutes2,900-3,000 wordsDeep-dive video essay, webinar

Two rules when you're over budget: cut the intro before anything else, and cut adjectives before you cut examples. The payoff and CTA are the last things to go — a tight script that delivers beats a thorough script that rambles, every time.

The 12 Free Video Script Templates

Each template below lists what it's best for, its runtime and word budget, and the structure to fill in. The first two include complete worked examples so you can see the word budget in action.

Template 1: The 15-Second Short (Hook-Value-CTA)

Best for: YouTube Shorts, TikTok, Reels — one tip, one tool, one moment. Runtime: 10-20 seconds. Structure: hook → one idea → one CTA. No intro, no "quick backstory", no second idea.

Worked example (38 words, ~15 seconds)

HOOK (0-2s): "You're overthinking your Shorts scripts."

VALUE (2-12s): "Line one: name the problem. Line two: the answer in one sentence. Line three: one action for the viewer. That's the whole template."

CTA (12-15s): "Save this, and script your next three Shorts in ten minutes."

Template 2: The 60-Second Problem-Solution Short

Best for: Shorts, TikTok, Reels where you need a little proof before the CTA. Runtime: 30-60 seconds, 75-180 words. Structure: hook → problem (1 sentence) → solution (2-3 beats) → proof (1 sentence) → CTA.

Worked example (110 words, ~40 seconds)

HOOK (0-2s): "Your invoices are late because you're sending them late — here's the fix."

PROBLEM: "Most freelancers invoice after the project ends, which means 30 extra days of waiting, every single time."

SOLUTION: "Invoice at the halfway mark instead. Send the first half when the work starts, the second half at delivery. Build it into your contract so it's not a negotiation — it's the default."

PROOF: "I cut my average payment time from 34 days to 11 doing exactly this."

CTA: "Follow for the email template I send with every halfway invoice."

Template 3: The Listicle Short (Carousel Format)

Best for: "5 signs", "3 tools", "4 mistakes" — numbered content in short-form. Runtime: 30-60 seconds. Structure: hook ("The 4 signs your pricing is too low") → items at 8-10 seconds each, best item LAST for retention → micro-CTA. Each item: claim (5 words) → one-sentence explanation. On-screen numbers do the counting so you don't waste spoken words on "number two is…".

Template 4: The Educational Tutorial (YouTube Long-Form)

Best for: how-to content, the highest-intent search traffic on YouTube. Runtime: 8-12 minutes. Structure: cold open showing the finished result (15s) → promise ("by the end you'll have X") → prerequisites (30s) → steps in order, one beat per step, chapter transition between each → common mistakes to avoid → recap → CTA. Show the result before teaching it — proof first, process second is the single biggest retention lever in tutorial scripts.

Template 5: The Listicle / Ranking (Long-Form)

Best for: "7 best tools for X", "top 5 strategies" — search-friendly, bingeable. Runtime: 10-15 minutes. Structure: hook teasing the #1 item → rules of the list (criteria, 20s) → items counting DOWN from lowest to highest ranked → for each item: what it is, who it's for, one limitation, one pro tip → #1 item gets double the time of any other → CTA. Counting down (not up) is what makes viewers stay to the end — the template builds the retention in.

Template 6: The Product Review

Best for: affiliate content, "honest review" searches. Runtime: 8-12 minutes. Structure: verdict up front ("after 60 days, here's who should buy this and who shouldn't") → quick specs → 3 tested claims, shown not stated → who it's for → who it's NOT for → the honest flaw (the credibility beat that makes the whole review trusted) → alternatives in one sentence each → verdict restated → CTA. Stating the verdict at the start and end is the review template's signature move — it respects the viewer and still retains.

Template 7: The Video Sales Letter (VSL)

Best for: selling a product or service from a single video. Runtime: 4-8 minutes, 600-1,200 words. Structure: pattern-interrupt hook → problem, in the viewer's own words → agitation (cost of not solving it) → your story in 3 sentences (why you built this) → the mechanism (why this works when others don't) → offer stack (what they get, itemised) → proof (results, testimonials, screenshots) → guarantee → urgency (real scarcity or deadline) → single CTA. Every VSL script template is a variant of this ten-beat order; the pitch fails at whichever beat you skip.

Template 8: The 30-Second Pre-Roll Ad

Best for: YouTube/Instagram ads, paid placement. Runtime: 15-30 seconds, 40-75 words. Structure: visual pattern-interrupt (0-2s) → problem in the viewer's words → product as the mechanism, shown in use → one differentiator → CTA with a reason to act now. Ads are scripts compressed to their minimum: show the product by second 5, name the brand by second 10.

Template 9: The Sponsored Integration Segment

Best for: mid-roll sponsor reads in podcasts and YouTube videos. Runtime: 60-90 seconds, 150-220 words. Structure: natural transition from content ("this episode's workflow actually runs on…") → what the product does in one sentence → genuine personal use story → the offer (code, link) → hand back to content. The script template matters more here than anywhere: the segment must feel like the show, not an ad break — matching the host's voice is a structural requirement, not a nice-to-have.

Template 10: The Expert Interview

Best for: podcast-style video, guest content. Runtime: 20-40 minutes, but the script is short — you script the frame, not the answers. Structure: cold-open clip of the best 20 seconds of the interview → host setup: who the guest is and why you should care (30s) → question ladder: background → process → failure story → contrarian take → rapid-fire → the one question the audience must answer in comments → CTA. Script the first question tightly — the interview's energy is set in the first two minutes.

Template 11: The Brand Story (Before-After-Bridge)

Best for: founder content, about pages, launch videos. Runtime: 60-90 seconds. Structure: before (the world or you, without the product — make it specific) → the turning point (one moment, one sentence) → after (the changed state) → bridge (the product as the vehicle) → soft CTA ("if that's where you are too…"). The video script template keeps the story under 90 seconds because brand stories die at the 2-minute mark — the format forces compression.

Template 12: The Course Lesson Module

Best for: online courses, onboarding videos, training content. Runtime: 5-10 minutes per lesson. Structure: lesson outcome stated as a capability ("by the end you'll be able to X") → why this matters in the course journey → concept (1-2 minutes max) → worked example → the learner does it (a pause-the-video exercise) → common mistakes → recap of the capability → next lesson bridge. Teaching scripts live or die on the pause-and-do exercise — passive lessons finish at 30% completion, lessons with a task finish near 90%.

Two-Column vs Single-Column: Which Video Script Format to Use

Once your video script template is filled in, you lay it out in one of two formats — and the choice is practical, not stylistic:

FormatBest forWhy
Two-column A/V (audio left, visuals right)B-roll-heavy videos, demos, ads, sponsored content, anything a client approvesForces you to plan every shot before the edit; runtime can be counted row by row
Single-column (spoken lines, bracketed visual notes)Talking-head videos, teleprompter work, podcastsFaster to write, reads more naturally on camera, teleprompter-friendly

The A/V format is the professional standard for a reason: when the right side of a row says what the viewer sees at that moment, editing stops being improvisation. Use single-column for anything you film yourself in one take; switch to two-column the moment a second person touches the project.

How to Use a Video Script Template (5 Steps)

  1. Pick the template that matches the job — teaching, entertaining, or selling. The 12 above cover the formats; choosing "tutorial" when the video is really a VSL is the most common misstep.
  2. Write the hook first, before anything else, using the 12 formulas. If the hook isn't strong, nothing downstream fixes it.
  3. Fill the beats to the word budget from the runtime table — write tight from the start instead of editing down later.
  4. Read it aloud, timed. Spoken pace is slower than reading pace, and every script has one sentence that trips the tongue. Cut 10-15% on this pass.
  5. Mark the visuals: note where on-screen text, B-roll, or demo shots go — in the A/V column or brackets. A script without visual marks is a script the edit will fight.

5 Scripting Mistakes That Kill Retention

  1. The "hey guys" intro. Every second before the hook is a second of lost viewers. Brand your intro with a visual, not 8 spoken words.
  2. Multiple ideas in a short-form script. One Short = one idea. The algorithm measures retention per idea, not per creator.
  3. Two CTAs. "Subscribe and comment and click the link" is three asks. One action per video; put the rest in the description or pinned comment.
  4. Scripting to impress instead of to deliver. Clever phrasing that costs comprehension loses retention. The script is a delivery mechanism for the payoff, not a writing showcase.
  5. Never reading aloud. The single most common cause of reshoots. If you stumble on a line, your viewers stumble on it too.
