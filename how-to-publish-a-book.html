How to Publish a Book Independently (2026 Guide)

Published September 2026 · 12 min read

Publishing a book independently means you own every decision — editing, cover, price, platform — and keep the majority of every sale: up to 70% of the list price on Amazon Kindle versus the roughly 10% per copy a traditional contract typically pays out. The full path is eight steps: validate the market, write to a schedule, edit in four passes, format for Kindle and print, design a cover that survives the thumbnail test, upload to KDP with optimised metadata, price inside the 70% royalty tier, and launch with reviews and promos. Realistic cost: $0-$2,500. Realistic timeline: 4-9 months. This guide walks through every step with the actual numbers — what things cost, what royalties pay, and where first-time authors lose money.

Publishing a Book Independently: FAQs

How much does it cost to publish a book independently?

$0-$2,500 depending on what you do yourself. A fully DIY launch (self-edit, free formatting tools, DIY cover) costs almost nothing but time. A lean professional launch — professional copyediting, a premade cover, free formatting tools — typically runs $600-$1,600 for an 80,000-word book. A fully professional production with developmental editing and a custom cover runs $2,000-$5,000. The one expense worth keeping is professional copyediting: readers forgive a plain cover, but typos generate permanent 1-star reviews.

What is the best platform to publish a book independently?

The best platform for most first-time indie authors is Amazon KDP — free to use, roughly 70% of the ebook market, 70% royalty on books priced $2.99-$9.99, zero setup fee. Widen later: Apple Books and Kobo Writing Life both pay 70% with no fees, Draft2Digital aggregates the smaller stores and libraries for a ~10% cut, and IngramSpark adds bookstore and library print distribution. Go wide once you have a second book; start with KDP on your first.

How long does it take to publish a book independently?

4-9 months realistically: 8-12 weeks to draft at 1,500 words a day, 3-4 weeks of developmental feedback and beta readers, 2-3 weeks of copyediting, 1-2 weeks for formatting and cover, and 2-4 weeks of pre-launch setup (advance copies, pre-order, metadata). The upload to Amazon KDP goes live in under 72 hours — writing the book is the long part.

Do self-published authors actually make money?

Most first books sell 250-1,000 copies in year one — one book is rarely a living. Authors earning real income almost always have 3+ books in one genre, because read-through compounds: every reader who finishes book one buys book two. Per-unit math on Amazon: a $4.99 ebook earns roughly $3.30 per sale, a $9.99 ebook roughly $6.60, a $14.99 paperback roughly $6 after printing costs.

Do I need an ISBN to publish a book independently?

No. Kindle ebooks use Amazon's free ASIN, not an ISBN. For paperbacks, accept Amazon's free assigned ISBN inside KDP — the trade-off is that it's tied to that Amazon edition. Buy your own ISBN ($125 for one in the US from Bowker) only if you want to control the publisher of record or push the same print edition through IngramSpark, which requires you to supply an ISBN. UK/NZ ISBNs come from Nielsen; Australia uses Thorpe-Bowker.

How much should I price my self-published book?

$2.99 is the lowest price that makes sense on Kindle, because KDP pays a 70% royalty only between $2.99 and $9.99 — outside that band the royalty drops to 35%. At $1.99 you earn about $0.70 per sale; at $2.99 you earn about $1.95. Most indie novels sit at $3.99-$5.99, series books $5.99-$9.99. Price paperbacks to keep $2-$4 per unit after printing.

Is self-publishing worth it in 2026?

Yes, for most authors who aren't already famous. You keep up to 70% of list price, control the price and can change it in minutes, publish in weeks instead of 18-24 months, and keep all rights. The trade-offs: you pay for editing and covers yourself, bookstores are hard to reach without IngramSpark, and income only compounds with multiple books. Books that started indie include The Martian and Wool — proof the ceiling is high, even though the median outcome is modest.

What Independent Publishing Actually Means

Independent publishing (self-publishing) is when the author acts as their own publisher: you hire or perform the editing, commission or make the cover, choose the platforms, set the price, and keep the royalties. You are not "less published" — the book is genuinely published, sold worldwide, and eligible for bestseller lists and awards that accept indie titles. What changes is who does the work a traditional publisher would do, and who takes the margin. Traditional publishing pays an advance and roughly 10% of list per copy; independent publishing pays you up to 70% of list per copy and costs you the production budget. For anyone without a platform of tens of thousands of followers, it is usually the faster and higher-margin path to readers.

How to Publish a Book Independently: The 8 Steps

Step 1 — Validate the market before you finish the manuscript

The most common fatal mistake in independent publishing is writing a book nobody in its category is buying. Before (or while) you write, run a 60-minute Amazon audit: open the Kindle store, find the top 20 bestsellers in the category your book belongs to, and check three things. First, are several of the top 20 independently published? If yes, indie books sell there. Second, do the bestsellers have fewer than about 500 reviews? That means a new author can realistically compete. Third, do the covers and titles follow a consistent pattern? That tells you exactly what readers in this sub-genre expect to see. If the top 20 is wall-to-wall traditionally published blockbusters, drop one level to a more specific sub-category where indie books already sell — then write for that reader.

Step 2 — Write to a schedule, not to inspiration

A full-length novel runs 70,000-90,000 words; practical nonfiction runs 40,000-60,000. At 1,500 words a day, six days a week, a first draft takes 8-10 weeks. The structure that works is three phases: draft fast (never re-read yesterday's pages), revise for structure (plot holes, pacing, chapter order), then polish prose. Track a daily word count in a simple log — the streak itself keeps you writing. Momentum is a compounding asset; perfectionism on page 12 is where manuscripts die.

Step 3 — Edit in four passes (and never skip the last two)

  • Developmental edit: does the structure work? Plot holes, pacing, character motivation, chapter order.
  • Line edit: sentence-level flow, voice, repetition.
  • Copyedit: grammar, consistency, timeline errors — the professional expense worth paying for.
  • Proofread: final typos on the formatted file, not the Word document.

Between the developmental and copyedit passes, recruit 5-10 beta readers who actually read your genre and ask them three questions: where did you get bored, where were you confused, and would you pay for this? A copyedit for an 80,000-word book costs roughly $500-$1,500 (about 1-2 cents per word). This is the one cost that should survive every budget cut — a book with visible typos collects 1-star reviews that permanently cap its rating and its sales.

Step 4 — Format for Kindle and print

Readers get an EPUB for Kindle; you also want a print-ready PDF for the paperback. The free path is completely adequate: Kindle Create (Amazon's free tool) or Draft2Digital's free formatter produce clean results. One-time purchases Atticus (~$147) and Vellum (~$199, Mac) produce both ebook and print layouts and pay for themselves by book two. The formatting details that matter: chapters start about a third down the page, a consistent scene-break marker (***), and proper front and back matter — title page, copyright page, and critically a review-request page at the end of the book. That last page measurably lifts review volume, and reviews drive sales.

Step 5 — Design a cover that survives the thumbnail test

Your cover will be judged at roughly 100×160 pixels, because that is its size in Amazon search results. Five rules: (1) the title must be legible at thumbnail size; (2) use your genre's typography conventions — readers identify genre by typeface before they read a word; (3) one focal image, not a collage; (4) keep series branding consistent so books two and three are recognisable; (5) pass the greyscale test — if the cover loses contrast in black and white, it disappears on e-ink Kindles. The test that matters: place your cover next to the top five bestsellers in your category, shrink everything to thumbnail, and compare. If yours looks amateur next to them, readers will assume the writing is amateur too. Premade covers run $50-$150, custom covers $300-$700, and a careful DIY in Canva is workable for nonfiction but risky for fiction.

Step 6 — Upload to KDP and optimise the metadata

Create a free account at kdp.amazon.com, complete the tax interview, and upload the manuscript EPUB and cover. Then do the part most authors rush — the metadata:

  • Description: write it as sales copy — a hook line, three benefit bullets, social proof, and a call to action. It is your book's landing page.
  • Categories: choose the two most specific sub-categories available, never the broad parent category. Specific categories are where a new book can chart.
  • Keywords: use the seven slots for phrases a real reader would type — "slow burn fantasy romance with dragons" — not single words.

Enable every territory, set no DRM, and use a pre-order if your launch date is more than a week out: pre-orders concentrate sales into day one, which feeds Amazon's visibility algorithms. The book goes live within 72 hours.

Step 7 — Price inside the 70% royalty tier

Amazon KDP pays a 70% royalty on ebooks priced $2.99-$9.99 and only 35% outside that band. That makes $2.99 the lowest rational price on Kindle: at $1.99 you earn about $0.70 per sale; at $2.99 you earn about $1.95 — nearly triple per sale. Most indie novels sit at $3.99-$5.99, and series books at $5.99-$9.99. Paperbacks: KDP pays 60% of list minus printing cost — a 300-page paperback costs roughly $2.30 to print, so a $14.99 paperback nets you about $6.70 per copy. Royalties pay out about 60 days after month-end.

Step 8 — Launch with reviews, promos, and a 30-day plan

A book published without a launch plan sells to whoever stumbles on it. The plan that works:

  • 3-4 weeks before launch: send advance copies to 20-50 genre readers — your email list, reader Facebook groups, Goodreads friends — and ask for honest reviews in launch week. A book with 10+ reviews on day one converts browsers into buyers; a book with zero reviews stalls.
  • Launch week: email your list, post where your readers gather, run one or two newsletter promo placements (Fussy, Entitled-to-Opinion-style list promos; BookBub Featured if you can win the auction — one placement can move 1,000-5,000 copies).
  • Weeks 2-4: start low-bid Amazon Ads on exact-match keywords, run a $0.99 or free promo on book one if it's a series, and — the single highest-return activity — start writing book two.

Never buy, swap, or incentivise fake reviews: Amazon removes them and can close your account. The compounding truth of indie income is that the second and third books sell the first one — read-through is the engine, and it only exists if you keep writing.

How Much a Self-Published Book Earns: The Honest Math

Set expectations correctly and the business model works; set them wrong and book one "failing" ends the career before it starts. A first indie novel typically sells 250-1,000 copies in its first year. At a $4.99 price with roughly $3.30 per sale, that is $825-$3,300 — a start, not a living. The authors who earn consistent income share one pattern: three or more books in one genre. Read-through compounds — a reader who loves book one buys books two and three with no extra marketing cost. Practically: a 3-book series with 40% read-through and 500 monthly book-one sales at $4.99 earns roughly (500 × $3.30) + (200 × $3.30) + (80 × $3.30) ≈ $2,570/month — the same marketing spend, tripled output. This is why the best advice in independent publishing is unglamorous: finish book one, launch it properly, and immediately write book two.

7 Mistakes First-Time Indie Authors Make

  1. Publishing the first draft. No edit passes means 1-star reviews about "sloppy writing" that permanently cap the book's rating.
  2. A cover that fails at thumbnail size. Beautiful at full screen, illegible in Amazon search — where every sale begins.
  3. Pricing at $0.99 "to get sales." Falls off the 70% royalty tier; you earn a third of what $2.99 pays per copy.
  4. Ignoring metadata. Default categories, single-word keywords, and a description written as a plot summary instead of sales copy.
  5. Launching with zero reviews. Advance copies to 20-50 readers before launch day is the fix, and it costs nothing but postage and politeness.
  6. No series plan. One standalone book is a lottery ticket; three linked books are a business with read-through income.
  7. Quitting after book one underperforms. The first book's job is to start the backlist, not to buy the house. The compounding starts at book two.

The one-line version of this entire guide: write to a validated market, pay for a copyedit and a genre-correct cover, upload to KDP with specific categories and reader-language keywords, price at $2.99 or above, launch with reviews — then write the next book faster than this one.
