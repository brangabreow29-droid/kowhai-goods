What should a professional email signature include?

Five core elements: full name, job title, company name, phone number (if people should call you), and one link to your website. Optionally add one social profile or one CTA — never both. Keep it to 3-5 lines and under 100 words.

How big should an email signature be?

What Is a Professional Email Signature?

A professional email signature is a structured block at the end of every email you send: your name, role, company, and a way to reach or learn more about you. It is not a letterhead, not a business card dump, and not a place for personality quotes — it is the last line of every message and the most-viewed branding asset you own. A typical office worker attaches their signature to dozens of emails a day; over a year, that is thousands of impressions to colleagues, clients, prospects, and recruiters.

The definitive rule: a professional email signature should contain five core elements — name, job title, company, phone (if needed), and one website link — in 3-5 lines of text. Everything beyond that is optional and costs you attention. Recipients skim signatures in under two seconds; a signature that violates the size rule gets ignored exactly when it matters most, which is the moment someone decides whether to trust you.

Key takeaway: the best professional email signature is the one that survives Outlook, loads instantly, and gives the recipient exactly one next step.

How Big Should an Email Signature Be?

Signature size is where most DIY email signature builds fail, because the failure is invisible to the sender. The rules, with numbers:

  • Total length: 3-5 lines of text, under 100 words. Beyond five lines, recipients stop reading — and on long threads your signature starts to outweigh your message.
  • Total file size: under 15KB including images. Oversized signatures slow every send and some corporate spam filters penalise them.
  • Logo or headshot: display at 60-90 pixels tall, PNG under 10KB. Never use SVG — Outlook does not render SVG at all. Host the image at a stable URL; Gmail and Outlook both cache remote images.
  • Layout width: under 450 pixels. Phone screens render email at roughly 320-400 pixels wide; anything wider gets scaled or clipped.
  • Typography: maximum two fonts and two colours. Use a web-safe stack (Arial, Helvetica, Georgia, Verdana) at 11-13px — custom web fonts silently fall back to defaults in Outlook and Gmail anyway.

How to Add Your Signature in Gmail, Outlook, Apple Mail and Yahoo Mail

Once you've filled in a template, installation is a two-minute job in every major client. The one universal gotcha: Gmail, Outlook, and Apple Mail all want the rendered signature, not the HTML source code — you paste what the file looks like in a browser, not the markup.

Gmail

  1. Click the gear icon → See all settings → General tab.
  2. Scroll to the Signature section and click Create new.
  3. Open your signature HTML file in a browser, select the rendered signature, copy it, and paste into Gmail's editor. (Pasting raw source code into the box shows code as text — this is the most common failure.)
  4. Set it as the default for new emails and replies/forwards, then scroll to the bottom and click Save Changes.

Outlook (web and new Outlook desktop)

  1. Click the gear icon and search "signature" → open Compose and reply.
  2. Paste the rendered signature into the editor (Ctrl+A, Ctrl+C from the browser preview works).
  3. Tick Automatically include my signature on new messages (and replies if you want it there too) → Save.

Classic Outlook on Windows: File → Options → Mail → Signatures → New, then paste. If your pasted layout collapses, re-paste from a browser rather than from Word — Word adds its own markup that Outlook then re-mangles.

Apple Mail (macOS)

  1. Open Mail → Settings → Signatures and select your account.
  2. Click + to create a signature, name it, and drag it to the account's At random/In sequence list so it applies automatically.
  3. Apple Mail only accepts rich text: open your HTML file in Safari, select the signature, copy, and paste it into the signature box.

Yahoo Mail

  1. Click the gear icon → More Settings → Writing email.
  2. Toggle Signature on, paste the rendered signature into the editor, and save.

Why Email Signatures Break in Outlook (and How to Fix Yours)

If your email signature looks perfect in Gmail and falls apart in Outlook, the cause is structural: Outlook on Windows renders email HTML with Microsoft Word's layout engine instead of a browser. That engine ignores most modern CSS — and the list of what it silently drops is long:

  • Flexbox and CSS grid — layouts collapse into a single stacked column or overlap.
  • Absolute and relative positioning — positioned elements snap to unexpected places.
  • Web fonts — custom typefaces fall back to whatever Word picks.
  • SVG images — not rendered at all; use PNG or JPG.
  • Background images — unreliable; use solid background colours set on table cells.
  • Rounded corners and CSS effects — ignored in older builds.

The fix is the same five rules every professional email signature template (including the paid ones) is built on:

  1. Build the layout with nested tables, not CSS layout modules.
  2. Put CSS inline on every element — a <style> block alone is not enough, because Gmail strips or ignores parts of it.
  3. Set background colours on table cells (bgcolor / inline background-color), not with CSS backgrounds.
  4. Use PNG images with explicit width and height attributes, plus alt text for blocked-image scenarios.
  5. Stick to web-safe fonts and declare a full fallback stack.

Test before you roll out: send yourself the signature from Gmail to an Outlook address and a phone. Three seconds of rendering check catches 90% of signature bugs.

Dark Mode: Why Your Logo Looks Like a White Box

Dark mode is the newest way a "working" email signature silently fails. Outlook, Apple Mail, and Gmail on mobile can invert or dim background colours, and a logo saved on a white background becomes a glowing white rectangle floating in a dark email.

Three fixes, in order of effectiveness:

  • Save logos as transparent PNGs designed to read on both light and dark backgrounds — mid-tone colours survive both.
  • Never rely on white text for name or title; use dark text on light cells, and set the cell background explicitly so inversions can't surprise you.
  • Avoid pure black-and-pure-white designs — near-black (#1a1a1a) and off-white survive inversion far better.

7 Mistakes That Make an Email Signature Look Unprofessional

  1. The full-image signature. One PNG with everything baked in. Recipients can't copy your phone number, the image is blocked by default in many clients, and screen readers announce nothing. Covered above — always HTML text.
  2. The inspirational quote. "Sent with gratitude from a digital nomad" undermines every word above it. The signature is contact infrastructure, not a mood board.
  3. Two CTAs. "Book a call, download our deck, and follow us" is three asks. One action per signature; swap it when your priority changes.
  4. Five social icons. A row of every platform you've ever joined reads as clutter and none of them get clicked. One platform — the one your role actually uses.
  5. Copy-paste legal disclaimers in industries that don't require them. Finance, legal, and healthcare often must include them; everyone else just added four grey lines that no recipient has ever read.
  6. Animation and GIFs. They render inconsistently, distract from the message, and look dated rather than modern in a professional context.
  7. Leaving the default "Sent from my iPhone" on. The single most common signature mistake — it says you never set anything up. Mobile clients support full signatures now; add the same template you use on desktop.
