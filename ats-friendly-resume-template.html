ATS-Friendly Resume Template (2026): How to Get Past Applicant Tracking Systems

Published July 2026 · 12 min read

An ATS-friendly resume template is a resume format designed to be read correctly by applicant tracking systems — the software that over 90% of Fortune 500 companies and a growing majority of mid-size employers use to screen incoming applications. If your resume template uses two columns, a table, a header/footer for contact info, or graphics that a parser cannot read, the ATS scrambles or drops your content before a recruiter ever sees it. The template you choose matters more than the words on the page.

This guide explains what an ATS-friendly resume template is, why most popular templates fail parsing, the exact formatting rules that work in 2026, how to structure each section, and a ready-made ATS-optimised template you can download and start using in the next five minutes.

What Is an ATS-Friendly Resume Template?

An applicant tracking system (ATS) is software that employers use to collect, parse, search, filter, and rank job applications. When you submit a resume through an online portal — Workday, Greenhouse, Lever, Taleo, iCIMS, BambooHR, SmartRecruiters — it doesn't go straight to a recruiter's inbox. It goes into the ATS, which runs a process called parsing: extracting text from your file and sorting it into searchable database fields like Name, Job Title, Employer, Dates, Skills, Education.

If the parser can read your resume cleanly, your information lands in the right fields and you appear in recruiter searches. If the parser struggles — because your layout uses columns it reads left-to-right across rows, or tables it misinterprets, or graphics it can't OCR — your data lands in the wrong fields, or nowhere at all. You stop appearing in searches. Effectively, your resume disappears.

An ATS-friendly resume template is one built to make parsing easy. That means:

  • Single-column layout — the parser reads top-to-bottom, left-to-right, in a single flow. No sidebars, no two-column designs, no text boxes.
  • Standard section headings — "Work Experience," "Education," "Skills," "Summary." The parser uses these to categorise your content. Creative headings like "My Journey" or "What I Bring" get skipped.
  • Plain text formatting — standard fonts (Arial, Calibri, Georgia, Times New Roman), no text inside images, no icons, no charts, no graphics.
  • No tables or text boxes — tables scramble reading order; text boxes get skipped entirely.
  • Contact info in the body — not in the Word document header/footer, which parsers often ignore.
  • Standard date formats — "Jan 2024 – Present" or "January 2024 – March 2026." Avoid apostrophes in dates ('24) or non-standard separators.
  • .docx or .txt file — .docx is the safest format for ATS parsing. PDF works with most modern systems but can still cause issues if it's an image-based PDF or uses embedded fonts the parser can't map.
The resume that looks the most impressive to a human designer — columns, colour blocks, skill bars, icons — is usually the resume that parses the worst. The ATS-friendly template is deliberately plain. That's the point.

Why Most Popular Resume Templates Fail ATS Parsing

The templates that dominate Google search results, Pinterest, and Canva's homepage are designed to look good on screen. They use two-column layouts, sidebar contact panels, skill-level bars, icons for phone/email/location, coloured section dividers, and professional headshots. These are the exact elements that break ATS parsing.

Here are the specific failures we see most often:

1. Two-Column Layouts Scramble Reading Order

The most common template failure. A two-column layout puts contact info and skills in a left sidebar and experience in the main column. The parser reads left-to-right, top-to-bottom, so it reads the entire left sidebar first, then the entire right column. Your work experience ends up filed under "Skills," your dates get separated from their job titles, and the timeline the recruiter searches for is gone. Most parsers cannot reconstruct the intended reading order from a two-column layout.

2. Tables Break Date and Title Pairing

Templates that use tables to align dates with job titles look clean to a human. To a parser, a table is a grid of cells read in an order the parser chooses — often row by row, which separates the date from the title it belongs to. Jobscan's 2026 parsing analysis found table-based layouts were a top-five cause of misfiled work history. If your template uses a table for the experience section, replace it with plain text and tabs.

3. Text Boxes and Headers Get Skipped

Many modern templates place your name, phone, email, and LinkedIn in a Word document header or in a floating text box. Many ATS parsers do not read document headers, footers, or floating text boxes at all — they treat them as layout chrome, not content. Your contact info simply isn't parsed. The recruiter can't find your name in the database.

4. Graphics and Icons Are Invisible

A phone icon next to your number, an envelope next to your email, a small star rating next to a skill — none of these are text. The parser sees empty space. If your email address is only communicated via an icon plus text in a layout the parser reads as a graphic, it's lost. The same applies to skill-level bars: the parser sees the word "Photoshop" but not the 80% fill level, and may not even see "Photoshop" if it's embedded in a graphic element.

5. Non-Standard Fonts Cause Character Substitution

If your template uses a custom or decorative font that the parser's system doesn't have, characters can substitute or drop. This is rarer with modern parsers but still happens with embedded fonts in PDFs. Stick to Arial, Calibri, Georgia, Garamond, or Times New Roman — fonts installed on virtually every system.

6. Image-Based PDFs Can't Be Parsed at All

If you save your resume as a PDF from a design tool that rasterises the text (turns it into an image), the parser cannot extract anything. Some ATS systems OCR image PDFs, but the accuracy is poor — job titles get misspelled, dates get mangled, and you're filed incorrectly. Always export PDFs as text-based PDFs, or submit .docx.

The ATS-Friendly Resume Template: Section by Section

A template that passes ATS parsing follows a specific structure. Here is each section, what to include, and what the parser expects to find.

Contact Information

Place this at the very top of the document body, not in a header. Include your full name, phone, email, city and state (or city and country), and a LinkedIn URL. Use plain text, one line per item or separated by pipe characters. Do not include a photo — in most markets it's either ignored or, in the US, can trigger bias filters.

Professional Summary

Two to three lines under the heading "Summary" or "Professional Summary." State your title, years of experience, core skill, and one quantified achievement. This is where the parser picks up your primary job title keyword — make it match the job posting's title where honest.

Example: Senior Marketing Manager with 8 years driving B2B SaaS growth. Led paid acquisition to $4.2M ARR at 3.1x ROAS. Built and managed a team of six across content, paid, and lifecycle.

Skills

Use the heading "Skills" and list 10–15 relevant terms as a simple comma-separated line or a single-column bulleted list. Include the exact tools and technologies from the job description — if the posting says "Salesforce," write "Salesforce," not "SFDC" or "Salesforce CRM." Parsers match exact strings. Include both the tool name and the skill category (e.g., "Salesforce, HubSpot, Marketo — CRM and Marketing Automation").

Do not use skill-level bars, star ratings, or proficiency labels ("Expert," "Intermediate"). The parser cannot read these, and recruiters ignore them.

Work Experience

Use the heading "Work Experience" (or "Professional Experience"). For each role, list in this order, each on its own line:

  • Job Title — match the posting's title where honest
  • Company Name, City, State
  • Dates: Mon YYYY – Mon YYYY or Mon YYYY – Present
  • 3–5 bullet points starting with action verbs, each with a quantified result

The parser pairs your job title with the company and dates by reading order. Keep these on consecutive lines with no gaps, tables, or columns between them.

Education

Use the heading "Education." List degree, major, university, and graduation year. If you're more than three years into your career, keep this brief — one or two lines. The parser uses it to confirm degree requirements, not to evaluate you.

Certifications (optional)

Use the heading "Certifications" and list relevant credentials with their year. Many ATS systems index certifications as a separate searchable field, so including the exact cert name (e.g., "AWS Certified Solutions Architect – Associate (2024)") helps you appear in filtered searches.

How to Match Keywords Without Keyword Stuffing

ATS systems rank candidates by keyword relevance — how often and how prominently the terms from the job description appear in your resume. But simply listing "project management" fifteen times hurts you when a human reads it, and modern ATS systems detect and penalise obvious stuffing.

The approach that works: read the job posting, identify the 8–12 priority terms (skills, tools, methodologies, certifications), and weave each one into a bullet point where you actually used it. If the posting mentions "Agile," your resume should have a bullet like Led Agile ceremonies for two cross-functional teams, reducing cycle time from 4 weeks to 2.5 weeks. That's a keyword in context with a quantified result — the parser sees "Agile," the recruiter sees proof.

Include both singular and plural variants where natural (dashboard/dashboards, report/reports). Spell out acronyms on first use with the acronym in parentheses: Search Engine Optimization (SEO). This covers both the spelled-out and acronym forms of the keyword.

How to Test Your Resume for ATS Compatibility

You don't have to guess whether your resume parses. Three free methods:

  1. Copy-paste test. Open your .docx in Microsoft Word, select all, copy, and paste into Notepad or TextEdit (plain text mode). What you see is approximately what the parser sees. If your work history reads in the correct order with dates attached to the right roles, you're good. If it's scrambled or missing, your template has a parsing problem.
  2. Free ATS scanners. Jobscan and Resume Worded both offer free ATS scans that check your resume against a specific job description and flag parsing risks (missing fields, scrambled sections, low keyword match). The free tiers are limited but enough for a single application.
  3. Google Docs test. Upload your resume to Google Drive and open it in Google Docs. If the layout breaks or text reflows strangely, that's a sign the structure relies on Word-specific features that parsers may also struggle with.

Run the copy-paste test first — it catches 80% of parsing issues in five seconds and costs nothing.

Free vs Paid ATS-Friendly Resume Templates

The free ATS-friendly templates from Microsoft Word's built-in gallery, Google Docs template gallery, and resume.io's free tier are functional but have limitations: they're generic, used by millions of applicants (no differentiation when a recruiter does read them), and often locked to specific formatting you can't fully customise. Some free templates from job boards include hidden tracking or advertising.

A paid template bundle typically gives you multiple format variants (chronological, functional, hybrid), matching cover letter templates, a reference page, and detailed guidance on keyword optimisation and section ordering. The advantage isn't the template itself — any single-column .docx works — it's having a tested, clean structure plus the supporting material that saves you an hour of formatting.

The Bottom Line on ATS-Friendly Resume Templates

The template matters more than people think. A beautifully designed resume that doesn't parse is invisible. A plain, single-column, standard-heading resume that parses cleanly gets into the recruiter's search results — and that's the only way it gets read. The ATS-friendly template is not about looking generic; it's about being machine-readable so a human actually gets the chance to evaluate you.

If you take one thing from this guide: run your current resume through the copy-paste test. If it comes out scrambled, the template is the problem. Switch to a single-column, standard-heading format and resubmit. That single change is the difference between appearing in searches and not.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is an ATS-friendly resume template?

An ATS-friendly resume template is a resume format designed to be read correctly by applicant tracking system software. It uses a single-column layout, standard section headings (Summary, Skills, Work Experience, Education), plain-text formatting with no tables or graphics, and contact information placed in the document body rather than headers or footers. The goal is clean parsing so your information lands in the correct searchable database fields.

Do ATS systems reject resumes automatically?

Most ATS systems do not auto-reject resumes based on parsing alone. They parse your content into a database and rank or filter it by keyword match and recruiter criteria. The common "75% of resumes are rejected by ATS before a human sees them" statistic traces back to a 2012 study by a defunct resume-services company and is widely considered unreliable. However, a poorly parsing resume gets filed incorrectly or missing data, which has the same practical effect — it doesn't appear in recruiter searches, so it's never reviewed.

Is PDF or Word better for ATS?

.docx is the safest format for ATS parsing because it's the most universally readable. Text-based PDFs (exported from Word or Google Docs, not scanned) work with most modern ATS systems including Workday, Greenhouse, and Lever. Avoid image-based PDFs created by design tools — the parser cannot extract text from them. When in doubt, submit .docx unless the employer specifically requests PDF.

Can I use a two-column resume template with an ATS?

Not reliably. Two-column templates are the single most common cause of ATS parsing failures because the parser reads left-to-right across both columns, scrambling your reading order. Your skills sidebar gets read first, your work experience second, and the pairing between job titles, companies, and dates breaks. Stick to a single-column layout for any application going through an ATS.

What fonts are ATS-friendly?

Standard system fonts installed on virtually every computer: Arial, Calibri, Cambria, Georgia, Garamond, Helvetica, and Times New Roman. Avoid custom, decorative, or script fonts — if the parser's system doesn't have the font, characters can substitute or drop. Size between 10pt and 12pt for body text, 14pt–16pt for your name.

How do I know if my resume is ATS-friendly?

Run the copy-paste test: open your resume in Word, select all, copy, and paste into a plain text editor like Notepad. What appears is roughly what the parser sees. If your content reads in the correct order with dates attached to the right roles and no missing sections, your resume is ATS-friendly. You can also use free ATS scanners like Jobscan or Resume Worded to check against a specific job description.

Should I tailor my resume for each job application?

Yes. ATS systems rank candidates by keyword relevance to the specific job description. Tailoring means identifying the 8–12 priority terms in the posting and ensuring each appears in context in your resume. This typically takes 10–15 minutes per application and significantly improves your ranking in recruiter searches. A generic resume sent to 50 postings will outperform a tailored resume sent to 5 — but a tailored resume sent to 15 outperforms both.

How long should an ATS-friendly resume be?

One page for under 8 years of experience, two pages for 8+ years. ATS parsers handle multi-page resumes fine — the length concern is readability for the recruiter who reviews it post-parse, not parsing itself. Do not shrink fonts or margins to fit one page; 10pt body text with 0.5-inch margins is the minimum for clean parsing and human readability.
