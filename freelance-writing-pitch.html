FAQ: Freelance Writing Pitch

What is a freelance writing pitch?

A freelance writing pitch is a short email or message sent to a potential client or editor proposing your writing services or a specific article idea. It includes who you are, what you want to write, why you're qualified, and links to relevant writing samples. The best pitches are under 200 words, personalized to the recipient, and end with a clear call to action.

How do you write a freelance writing pitch that gets a response?

To write a freelance writing pitch that gets a response: research the client first, address them by name, lead with a specific article idea or service offer, prove relevance with 1-2 niche writing samples, keep it under 200 words, and end with a single clear question. Personalized pitches get 3-5x more replies than generic ones.

How long should a freelance writing pitch email be?

A freelance writing pitch email should be 150-200 words. Editors and content managers read pitches in under 15 seconds. Lead with your idea in the first two sentences, prove credibility with one or two relevant samples, and close with a single question. Long pitches get skimmed and deleted.

What should I put in the subject line of a freelance pitch?

The best freelance pitch subject lines are specific and benefit-driven. Use formats like "Freelance Pitch: [Article Headline]" for guest posts, or "Content for [Company Name]: [Your Niche] Writer Available" for cold pitches. Avoid vague subject lines like "Hello" or "Writing for you" — they look like spam.

How much does a freelance writer charge per article?

Freelance writers charge $50-$500+ per article depending on experience, niche, and word count. Beginner rates range from $0.05-$0.10 per word ($50-$100 for a 1,000-word article). Experienced niche writers charge $0.25-$0.50+ per word ($250-$500+ per article). B2B and SaaS writers command the highest rates. Use a freelance rate calculator to set your price confidently.

What is the best pitch template for freelance writers?

The best freelance writing pitch template follows a proven structure: personalized greeting, one-sentence hook referencing their recent work, your relevant qualification, a specific article idea or service offer, 1-2 niche writing sample links, and a single closing question. The Freelance Writer's Pitch Playbook includes 35+ tested templates covering cold outreach, guest posts, follow-ups, referrals, and re-pitches.

Should I follow up on a freelance writing pitch?

Yes. Follow up on a freelance writing pitch 5-7 days after sending if you haven't heard back. One follow-up increases response rates by 18-30%. Keep it to 2-3 sentences: reference your original pitch, add one new piece of value (a relevant sample or idea), and ask if they're interested. Don't follow up more than twice on the same pitch.

The Anatomy of a Winning Freelance Writing Pitch

Every pitch that gets a reply — regardless of niche, client type, or experience level — shares the same six-part structure. Master this and you can adapt it to any situation:

  1. Personalized greeting — Address the editor or content manager by name. "Hi Sarah" beats "Dear Editor" every time. Find the name on the About page, LinkedIn, or the publication's masthead.
  2. Hook (1 sentence) — Reference something specific about their recent work: a recent article they published, a company milestone, or a gap you noticed in their content. This proves you've done your research and aren't mass-emailing.
  3. Credibility (1-2 sentences) — Who you are and why you're qualified. Lead with your niche, not your resume. "I'm a SaaS content writer published in [Outlet]" is stronger than "I've been writing for five years."
  4. The offer (1-2 sentences) — A specific article idea with a working headline and brief description, or a clear service offer. Specificity is what separates a pitch from a greeting card.
  5. Proof (1-2 links) — Links to 1-2 writing samples in the same niche. Don't send attachments — they trigger spam filters. Don't send more than 3 — it creates decision paralysis.
  6. Call to action (1 question) — End with a single, easy-to-answer question: "Is this something you'd be interested in?" or "Would you be open to a 10-minute call next week?" One question, not three.
The #1 reason freelance pitches fail isn't bad writing — it's lack of specificity. A pitch that says "I'd love to write for you" gets deleted. A pitch that says "I noticed your blog covers [Topic] but doesn't have anything on [Specific Subtopic] — here's a 1,200-word outline" gets a reply.

7 Freelance Writing Pitch Templates You Can Use Today

Here are seven templates for the most common pitching scenarios. Each is under 200 words, structured for maximum response rate, and includes bracketed placeholders you replace with your own details. Copy, customize, and send.

Template 1: Cold Outreach Pitch (For Content Clients)

Use this when pitching a business that publishes a blog but hasn't posted recently, or when you want to offer ongoing content services. This is the highest-volume pitch type — send 10-20 per week for best results.

Subject: Content for [Company Name] — [Your Niche] writer available Hi [First Name], I've been following [Company Name]'s blog and really enjoyed your recent post on [specific topic from their blog]. One thing I noticed: you don't have content covering [specific gap/subtopic] — and that's an area where your competitors are publishing weekly. I'm a [your niche] freelance writer, and my work has appeared in [relevant outlet 1] and [relevant outlet 2]. I specialize in [your sub-niche] and could write a 1,200-word piece on [specific topic idea with working headline] for you. Here are two relevant samples of my work: - [Link to sample 1 — same niche] - [Link to sample 2 — same niche] Would you be open to a quick 10-minute call next week to discuss whether a trial article makes sense? Best, [Your Name] [Your website/portfolio link]

Template 2: Guest Post Pitch (For Publications)

Use this when pitching a specific article idea to an online publication, magazine, or blog. Publications want fully-formed ideas, not vague suggestions. Lead with the headline.

Subject: Freelance Pitch: [Working Headline] Hi [Editor Name], I'd love to pitch an article for [Publication Name]. Working headline: [Your Headline — make it specific and clickable] Brief: A [word count]-word piece covering [specific angle/takeaway]. It would include [expertise/insight you bring], [data/source type you'll use], and [practical takeaway for readers]. I haven't seen this angle covered on your site, and your audience of [target reader] would find it useful because [specific reason]. I'm a [your niche] writer published in [Outlet 1] and [Outlet 2]. Two relevant samples: - [Link 1] - [Link 2] If you're interested, I can have a full draft to you by [date — 7-10 days out]. If I don't hear back by [date], I'll assume it's a pass and pitch it elsewhere. Thanks for your time, [Your Name] [Portfolio link]

Template 3: The Follow-Up Pitch (After No Response)

Send this 5-7 days after your original pitch if you haven't received a reply. One follow-up can increase your response rate by 18-30%. Add new value — don't just ask "did you see my email?"

Subject: Re: [Original subject line] Hi [First Name], Just following up on my pitch from last week about [article topic/idea]. I wanted to add one thing: I just published a new piece on [related topic] for [outlet] — here's the link: [link]. It covers [brief one-line summary] and I think it shows the tone and depth I'd bring to a piece for [Company/Publication]. Is this something you'd be interested in, or should I pitch it elsewhere? Best, [Your Name]

Template 4: Referral Pitch (When Someone Introduced You)

When a mutual contact introduces you to a potential client, mention the referral in the first line. Referral pitches convert 5-10x better than cold pitches because the trust barrier is already lowered.

Subject: [Mutual contact name] suggested I reach out Hi [First Name], [Mutual contact's name] suggested I connect with you — they mentioned [specific reason, e.g., "you're looking for a B2B SaaS writer for your content pipeline"]. I'm a [your niche] freelance writer, and I've worked with [client/outlet 1] and [client/outlet 2] on [type of content]. [Mutual contact] thought my experience in [specific sub-niche] would be a good fit for what you're building at [Company Name]. Two samples that show my approach: - [Link 1] - [Link 2] Would you be open to a brief call this week to see if there's a fit? [Mutual contact] speaks highly of your team and I'd love to learn more about your content goals. Best, [Your Name] [Portfolio link]

Template 5: Re-Pitch After Rejection (Same Publication)

Getting rejected doesn't mean the door is closed. Editors respect persistence if it's done well. Wait 2-4 weeks, then pitch a new idea — better than the first. Acknowledge the prior pitch briefly, then move on.

Subject: Freelance Pitch: [New Headline] Hi [Editor Name], Thanks again for the feedback on my last pitch about [previous topic] — I appreciated the note about [specific feedback they gave]. I've got a different idea I think is a better fit: Working headline: [New Headline] Brief: [word count] words on [specific angle]. This would cover [key point 1], [key point 2], and [practical takeaway]. I noticed your audience of [reader type] engages most with [content type] — this piece follows that pattern. Two samples in this style: - [Link 1] - [Link 2] Would this angle work better for [Publication Name]? Best, [Your Name]

Template 6: LinkedIn Pitch (For Content Managers)

LinkedIn is the best channel for B2B pitching. Engage with their content first (comment on 2-3 posts over a week), then send a connection request with a short note, followed by this message once they accept.

Hi [First Name], Thanks for connecting! I've been enjoying your posts on [specific topic they post about] — especially the one about [specific post you commented on]. I'm a [your niche] content writer, and I help companies like [company type] produce [content type — e.g., "thought leadership blog posts that rank for competitive B2B keywords"]. I noticed [Company Name] is publishing [observation about their content], and I think I could help with [specific gap or opportunity]. I've written for [client/outlet 1] and [client/outlet 2] — here are two samples: - [Link 1] - [Link 2] Would a 15-minute call next week make sense to see if there's a fit? [Your Name] [Portfolio link]

Template 7: Pitch With No Published Samples (For Beginners)

No clips? No problem. Create 2-3 sample articles on Medium or a self-published blog in your target niche, then pitch using those. Editors care about writing quality, not where it was published. Be honest about being early in your career — frame it as enthusiasm, not a weakness.

Subject: Freelance Pitch: [Headline] Hi [Editor Name], I'd love to pitch an idea for [Publication Name]. Working headline: [Your Headline] Brief: A [word count]-word piece on [specific topic]. It would cover [key angle], [data/insight type], and [practical takeaway for readers]. I haven't seen this covered on your site and your audience of [reader type] would find it actionable. I'm a new [your niche] writer building my portfolio. I don't have published clips yet, but I've written two sample pieces in this style so you can see my approach: - [Link to Medium post 1] - [Link to Medium post 2] I can deliver a polished draft by [date]. If it's not a fit, I'd genuinely appreciate any feedback — I'm committed to improving. Would this angle interest you? Best, [Your Name] [Portfolio or LinkedIn link]

Freelance Pitch Subject Line Formulas That Get Opened

The subject line determines whether your pitch is read or deleted. After analyzing open rates across thousands of freelance pitches, these formulas consistently outperform the rest:

Scenario Subject Line Formula Why It Works
Guest post pitch Freelance Pitch: [Working Headline] Signals intent immediately — editors can triage it
Cold outreach Content for [Company]: [Niche] writer available Company name + niche = relevant at a glance
Referral pitch [Referrer's name] suggested I reach out Trust transfer — opens rates are 60%+
Follow-up Re: [Original subject line] Continues the thread — keeps context visible
LinkedIn DM (No subject — lead with the hook) Conversational — matches the platform's tone

Avoid generic subject lines like "Hello," "Writing for you," or "Freelance writer." They look like spam and get deleted before they're opened. Always include the company name or publication name — it signals the pitch is personalized, not mass-sent.

How to Find the Right Clients to Pitch

Sending 100 generic pitches to random companies will get you 0-2 replies. Sending 20 well-researched pitches to the right clients will get you 4-6. Targeting matters more than volume. Here's how to find clients worth pitching:

1. Look for companies already publishing content

A company with an active blog already values content. Check their blog — if they publish weekly or biweekly, they need writers. Companies with no blog are a harder sell because you have to convince them content matters before you can even pitch your services.

2. Search for companies in your specific niche

Google "list [your niche] companies" or "top [your niche] SaaS tools" to find prospect lists. AI search tools can generate categorized lists instantly. Filter for companies with blogs and active content marketing — those are your warmest prospects.

3. Check LinkedIn for content managers and editors

Search LinkedIn for "content manager [company name]" or "editor [publication name]." Engage with their posts for a week before pitching — comment thoughtfully on 2-3 pieces. When you do pitch, reference those interactions. Warm pitches outperform cold ones by 3-5x.

4. Use job boards as a reverse prospecting tool

When a company posts a freelance writing job but you'd rather pitch them directly, find the hiring manager on LinkedIn and pitch proactively. Companies that post writing jobs are actively investing in content — they're primed to receive pitches.

Common Freelance Pitch Mistakes That Kill Response Rates

1. Pitching too long

If your pitch is over 250 words, it won't be read. Editors scan pitches in 10-15 seconds. If they can't find your idea, your samples, and your question in that time, they move on. Cut every sentence that doesn't prove you're the right writer for the job.

2. Sending attachments

Attachments trigger spam filters, especially PDFs. Editors don't want to download files from strangers. Use links instead — to your portfolio, your published samples, or self-published pieces on Medium. Links are clickable, trackable, and safe.

3. Generic greetings

"Dear Sir/Madam" and "To Whom It May Concern" signal a mass email. Take two minutes to find the editor's name — it's on the About page, LinkedIn, or the publication's masthead. A personalized greeting alone can double your reply rate.

4. Not having niche-specific samples

A SaaS client doesn't care about your parenting blog posts. A finance publication doesn't care about your travel writing. Create 2-3 samples in your target niche before you start pitching. If you don't have published clips, self-publish them on Medium — editors care about writing quality, not the platform.

5. Asking too many questions

End with ONE question. "Would you be interested in this idea?" is easy to answer yes or no. "What's your budget? What topics do you need? When can we talk? What's your process?" is overwhelming. Reduce friction — make it effortless to reply.

How Many Pitches Should You Send Per Week?

The math is straightforward. If you send 10 personalized pitches per week with a 10-15% response rate, that's 1-2 new conversations weekly. Over a month, that's 4-8 potential clients in your pipeline. Not all will convert, but even a 25% close rate means 1-2 new clients per month — enough to build a sustainable freelance writing business.

Here's a realistic pitching schedule for freelance writers at different stages:

Stage Pitches/Week Expected Responses Goal
Beginner (no clients) 15-20 1-3 per week Land first 1-3 clients
Building (3-5 clients) 5-10 1-2 per week Replace low-paying clients
Established (5+ clients) 3-5 1 per week Maintain pipeline, raise rates

The key is consistency. Sending 5 pitches every week for 12 weeks (60 total) will outperform sending 30 pitches in one frantic week and then stopping for two months. Pitching is a pipeline activity — it pays off 4-8 weeks after you send, not immediately.

The 5-Day Pitching Sprint

If you've been stuck in preparation mode — polishing your portfolio, rewriting your bio, researching clients but never actually hitting send — this 5-day sprint will get you unstuck. Each day takes 30-60 minutes.

Day 1: Define your niche and create 3 samples

Pick one niche. Write three 800-1,200 word sample articles in that niche and publish them on Medium or a simple portfolio site. These are your pitching currency — without them, every pitch is weaker.

Day 2: Build a list of 20 target clients

Search for companies in your niche with active blogs. For each, find the content manager or editor's name and email. Use LinkedIn, About pages, and Hunter.io. Record everything in a spreadsheet — company, contact name, email, recent article they published, and a gap you noticed in their content.

Day 3: Write 5 pitch emails using Template 1

Using the cold outreach template above, write 5 personalized pitches. Each should reference a specific recent article from the client's blog and propose one specific article idea. Don't send them yet — let them sit overnight.

Day 4: Review, refine, and send

Read your 5 pitches with fresh eyes. Cut anything unnecessary. Make sure each is under 200 words and ends with one question. Then send all 5. Use a tracking tool (HubSpot's free email tracker, or Mailtrack) to see opens.

Day 5: Follow up on yesterday's pitches and write 5 more

Send a second batch of 5 pitches to new prospects. This builds your pipeline. By the end of the sprint, you'll have 10 pitches in the wild and a repeatable system you can run weekly.

How to Price Your Freelance Writing (So You Don't Undersell)

Even the best pitch won't help if you don't know what to charge when they say yes. Most freelance writers underprice by 30-50% because they don't have a rate framework. Here's the quick version:

Calculate your target annual income, divide by the number of billable hours you can realistically work (typically 60% of your working hours — the rest goes to admin, marketing, and pitching), then add 30% for taxes and business expenses. That's your hourly floor. For per-article pricing, multiply your hourly rate by the estimated hours to complete the piece, including research and revisions.
