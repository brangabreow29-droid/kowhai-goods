How to Start a Newsletter in 2026: The Complete Step-by-Step Guide

Published August 2026 · 12-minute read · Category: Writing & Publishing

An email newsletter is the only audience channel you fully own. Social platforms change their algorithms, throttle your reach, and can deplatform you overnight — but an email list is yours. You can send to it on your schedule, with your formatting, and no intermediary decides who sees it. That is why over 400,000 people start a newsletter every year, and why the newsletter economy is now a billion-dollar market.

This guide shows you exactly how to start a newsletter from zero — choosing a niche, picking the right platform, writing your first five issues, getting your first 100 subscribers, and eventually monetizing. Whether you're a freelancer building a client pipeline, a creator tired of renting audience on social media, or a subject-matter expert who wants to monetize what you know, the steps below are the same.

Key takeaway: Starting a newsletter costs $0 and takes 30 minutes to set up. The hard part isn't the technology — it's committing to a niche, publishing consistently for 12 months, and resisting the urge to switch platforms before you've earned the right to be picky. Most newsletters fail because the creator quits in month 3, not because they picked the wrong tool.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I start a newsletter from scratch?

Pick a niche you can write about weekly, choose a platform (Substack for simplicity, Beehiiv for growth and monetization), create a landing page with a clear value promise, write your first 5 issues before launching, and promote through personal network, social media, and communities. Setup takes under 30 minutes — both Substack and Beehiiv are free to start.

What is the best platform to start a newsletter in 2026?

Beehiiv is the best newsletter platform in 2026 if you plan to monetize, because it charges a flat monthly fee instead of taking 10% of subscription revenue. Substack is best for maximum simplicity and built-in discovery. ConvertKit (now Kit) is strongest for automation and segmentation. Ghost is best for full ownership and self-hosting.

How much does it cost to start a newsletter?

It costs $0 to start a newsletter. Both Substack and Beehiiv offer free plans. Substack takes a 10% cut of paid subscriptions plus Stripe's 2.9% fee. Beehiiv's free plan supports up to 2,500 subscribers with no platform fees on paid subscriptions. A custom domain ($10-15/year) is optional but recommended.

How many subscribers do you need to make money?

You need approximately 1,000 engaged subscribers to start making meaningful money. At 1,000 subscribers with a 45% open rate, you can earn $50-150 per sponsored email. With paid subscriptions at $8-10/month and a 2-5% conversion rate, 1,000 free subscribers yield 20-50 paying subscribers ($200-500/month). Most creators combine sponsorships, paid subscriptions, and affiliate revenue.

How often should you send a newsletter?

Send your newsletter weekly for the first 12 months to build momentum and subscriber expectations. After 12 months with a stable base, biweekly can work. Daily is effective for news-roundup formats but requires a team or heavy automation. Consistency matters more than frequency — subscribers who expect you every Tuesday engage more than those who receive email at random intervals.

What should I write in my first newsletter?

Write a manifesto issue: who you are, why you started, what readers get by subscribing, and what to expect. Keep it under 800 words and end with a question that invites replies — replies train email algorithms to prioritize your future emails. The five best first-issue formats are the manifesto, best-of roundup, deep dive, interview, and how-to guide.

How do I get my first 100 newsletter subscribers?

Email 50-100 personal contacts individually (not a mass blast), add a newsletter link to all social bios, post about your launch in 3-5 relevant communities (Reddit, Slack, Discord), write guest posts for newsletters in adjacent niches, and create a free lead magnet gated by email signup. Most creators reach 100 subscribers within 2-4 weeks.

Step 1: Choose Your Newsletter Niche

Your niche is not "productivity" or "marketing." Those are categories, not niches. A niche is a specific intersection of audience, topic, and angle that gives you a defensible position. "Productivity tips for solo founders who use AI tools" is a niche. "Productivity" is a graveyard.

Use this five-criteria framework to validate your niche before you write a single issue:

  1. Can you write 50 issues without running out of ideas? If you can brainstorm 50 specific topics in 30 minutes, the niche has enough depth. If you're stuck at 10, it's too narrow or you don't know the topic well enough.
  2. Is there an existing audience spending money? Search Reddit, YouTube, and existing newsletters. If people are paying for courses, books, tools, or paid newsletters in this space, demand is proven.
  3. Do you have an unfair advantage? This could be professional expertise (10 years in fintech), access (you know the key players), or a unique angle (you're the contrarian voice in a crowded field).
  4. Can you name 3 newsletters already in this space? Competition is validation, not a threat. If no one is writing about it, there's usually a reason. You don't need to be the only newsletter — you need to be the best one for a specific reader.
  5. Does the unique-angle test pass? Fill in: "Unlike [existing newsletter], my newsletter is the only one that [specific differentiator]." If you can't complete this sentence convincingly, your angle isn't sharp enough.
The best newsletter niche is one where you have professional expertise, the audience already spends money on related content, and you can identify a specific angle that no existing newsletter owns. The most profitable newsletter niches in 2026 are B2B/professional (finance, software, marketing), health and wellness, and AI/technology — but a well-executed niche newsletter will always outperform a generic one.

Step 3: Set Up Your Landing Page

Your landing page is your sales pitch. Before you've written a single issue, this page must convince a visitor to hand over their email address. A weak landing page is the number one reason newsletters stall at 50 subscribers. Every element should answer one question: why should I give you my email?

Here's the anatomy of a high-converting newsletter landing page:

  • Headline: One sentence stating the specific outcome readers get. Not "Weekly thoughts on marketing" — instead: "Every Tuesday, one actionable marketing tactic you can implement before lunch."
  • Subheadline: Who it's for and who it's not for. "For B2B marketers at companies under 50 employees. Not for agency teams or enterprise."
  • What you get: 3-5 bullet points describing the content. Be specific: "A teardown of one marketing campaign," "The exact tools I used," "One template you can copy."
  • Cadence: State the schedule explicitly. "Every Tuesday at 8 AM. No bonus emails, no spam."
  • Social proof: If you have any — subscriber count, testimonials, notable mentions. If you have none, skip this. Don't fake it.
  • Email capture: One field (email only). Every additional field reduces conversion by 10-25%. Name is optional; anything else is a mistake.
  • "No spam" promise: Explicitly state you will never sell or share their email. This single line increases conversion by 5-15%.

Both Substack and Beehiiv generate landing pages automatically, but you should customize the copy rather than accepting the default. Spend 30 minutes writing the landing page before you launch — it's the highest-ROI 30 minutes you'll invest in your newsletter.

Step 4: Write Your First 5 Issues Before Launch

The single biggest mistake new newsletter creators make is launching with zero issues written. By week three, they're staring at a blank page, the deadline is tomorrow, and they either publish something rushed and bad or skip the issue entirely. Both kill momentum.

Write five issues before you announce your newsletter to anyone. This gives you a buffer so you're never publishing under panic, and it forces you to validate that you actually have enough to say. If you can't write five issues, your niche is too narrow or you don't know the topic well enough yet.

Here are five proven first-issue formats that work for any niche:

Issue 1: The Manifesto

Who you are, why you're starting this newsletter, what readers will get, and what makes your perspective different. Under 800 words. End with a question that invites replies — early replies signal quality to email algorithms and boost deliverability.

Issue 2: The Best-Of Resource Roundup

Curate the 7-10 best resources (tools, articles, books, podcasts) in your niche that you actually use. Add one sentence of commentary on each explaining why it's worth your reader's time. This type of issue gets forwarded and shared because it saves readers hours of research.

Issue 3: The Deep Dive

Take one specific topic in your niche and go deeper than anyone else has. If you're writing about freelance pricing, don't write "how to set your rate" — write "the exact math behind a $95/hour freelance rate, including taxes, benefits, and unpaid time." Specificity is what makes a newsletter worth paying for.

Issue 4: The Interview

Interview one person your readers care about. Five questions, published as Q&A. This leverages their audience (they'll share it), establishes your credibility by association, and gives you content without having to write 1,500 words from scratch.

Issue 5: The How-To Guide

Walk through a specific process step by step. "How I landed my first 3 freelance clients using cold email" or "How I set up my content calendar in 20 minutes." How-to issues are the most-saved and most-revisited format — they're the backbone of a newsletter that delivers ongoing value.

Step 5: Get Your First 100 Subscribers

The first 100 subscribers are the hardest. You have no social proof, no archive, no momentum. Here's the playbook that works repeatedly:

Phase 1: Personal Network (Days 1-7)

Email 50-100 people you know individually. Not a mass BCC blast — that gets ignored and feels spammy. A personal email to each person: "I'm starting a newsletter about [topic]. I think you'd find it useful because [specific reason]. Would you like to subscribe? No pressure if not." Expect a 30-50% yes rate. This gets you to 25-50 subscribers.

Phase 2: Social & Communities (Days 7-14)

Add your newsletter link to every social media bio. Post about your launch in 3-5 relevant communities — subreddits, Slack groups, Discord servers, Facebook groups. Don't just drop a link; write a value-first post that teaches something useful, then mention the newsletter at the end. This gets you to 50-80 subscribers.

Phase 3: Lead Magnet (Days 14-21)

Create a free resource gated by email signup. This could be a checklist, template, resource list, or mini-course related to your niche. Promote it the same way you'd promote a paid product. A well-targeted lead magnet can convert at 20-40% vs. 2-5% for a plain landing page. This pushes you past 100.

Phase 4: Cross-Promotion (Days 21-30)

Find newsletters with 500-2,000 subscribers in adjacent niches (not direct competitors). Email the creator proposing a cross-promotion: you mention their newsletter, they mention yours. This is the highest-quality subscriber source because the referral comes with a trust endorsement. Expect 20-50 new subscribers per cross-promotion.

Step 6: Grow from 100 to 1,000 Subscribers

Getting to 100 is about hustle. Getting from 100 to 1,000 is about systems. The tactics that got you to 100 don't scale — you can't email 500 people individually. Here's what works at this stage:

  • Referral program: Add a referral mechanic (Beehiiv has this built-in; Substack has a basic version). Offer a milestone reward: refer 3 friends, get a free resource; refer 10, get a 30-minute call. This turns your existing subscribers into a growth engine.
  • Repurpose every issue: Turn each newsletter into 3-5 social media posts, a LinkedIn carousel, or a short video. The newsletter is your content engine; social is the distribution arm.
  • Guest posts: Write for 1-2 larger newsletters per month. Offer valuable content, include a soft CTA to your newsletter in your bio. One guest post in a 10,000-subscriber newsletter can bring 200-500 new subscribers.
  • SEO your archive: If you're on a platform with public archives (Substack, Beehiiv, Ghost), each issue is a web page that can rank in search. Write issue titles as search queries: "How to calculate freelance hourly rate" beats "Rate math."
  • Recommendation networks: On Substack, actively recommend and get recommended by 5-10 newsletters in your niche. The recommendation flow can bring 10-30% of new subscribers automatically.

Sponsorship Pricing Formula

Price sponsorships using CPM (cost per thousand subscribers). The industry standard for newsletters is $50-250 CPM, depending on niche and engagement:

Sponsorship Price = (Subscribers × Open Rate × $CPM) / 1000

For example: 2,000 subscribers, 45% open rate, $100 CPM: (2000 × 0.45 × 100) / 1000 = $90 per sponsored email. Niche B2B newsletters command $150-250 CPM. General interest newsletters get $30-75 CPM. Always price on engaged subscribers (open rate), not total list size — sponsors who get clicks buy again.

Common Newsletter Mistakes to Avoid

  1. Switching platforms in month 2. You haven't earned the right to be picky yet. Stay on your first platform for at least 6 months or until you hit 1,000 subscribers. Platform migration is a distraction disguised as productivity.
  2. Writing for everyone. "A newsletter about business, productivity, and life" has no audience. Write for one specific person — the more specific, the more the right readers self-select.
  3. Inconsistent cadence. If you say "every Tuesday," send every Tuesday. Missing one issue signals to subscribers that this isn't a priority for you — and if it's not a priority for you, it's not for them either.
  4. Generic subject lines. "Issue #7: Weekly Update" gets a 15% open rate. "The $12 tool that replaced my entire marketing stack" gets a 55% open rate. Your subject line is 80% of the battle.
  5. Never asking for replies. Replies are the strongest deliverability signal. End every issue with a question. Even if only 5 people reply, those replies tell email providers your emails are wanted.
  6. Monetizing too early. Don't add a paywall at 200 subscribers. Build a free audience first, then gate premium content once you have 500+ engaged subscribers who already trust you.

Newsletter Analytics: What to Track

Don't drown in metrics. Track these five numbers weekly:

Metric Good Great What It Tells You
Open rate 35% 50%+ Subject line quality + sender reputation
Click rate 3% 8%+ Content relevance + CTA clarity
Reply rate 0.5% 2%+ Engagement depth + deliverability signal
Unsub rate <0.5% <0.2% Audience fit (high unsub = wrong audience)
Forward rate 0.1% 1%+ Virality + word-of-mouth growth

If your open rate drops below 30% for two consecutive issues, you have a deliverability problem, not a subject-line problem. Check that your domain authentication (SPF, DKIM, DMARC records) is set up correctly, and ask subscribers to move you to their primary inbox. Substack and Beehiiv handle most of this automatically, but it's worth verifying after any sustained open-rate drop.
